# Cover note: How accurate were old maps?

## Suitable journals

Checked online in 2026 (sources below). None of the four requires an article processing charge (APC) from the author as far as could be checked: the Journal of Historical Geography is confirmed hybrid, with open access an optional paid extra; Imago Mundi and The Cartographic Journal are Taylor and Francis subscription titles with optional paid open access, but their own pages could not be loaded to confirm this; e-Perimetron is a non-profit online journal whose home page mentions no fees. Confirm the current fee and length rules on each journal's own author page before submitting: several publisher pages could not be read from here, as noted per journal. The paper is about 7,100 words of main text before the references and about 9,250 words in all, including the abstract, references and appendix (abstract 240 words), with 6 figures and 3 main-text tables plus 3 appendix tables.

1. **Imago Mundi: The International Journal for the History of Cartography** (Routledge, Taylor and Francis; subscription journal with optional paid open access, not confirmed on the journal's own page). Published the Gough Map studies cited here (Lilley et al. 2009; Smallwood 2010; Solopova 2012; Delano-Smith et al. 2017). Best fit for the argument that accuracy follows purpose, not date. Word limit: not confirmed (the Taylor and Francis instructions page could not be loaded); check before submitting.
2. **The Cartographic Journal** (Taylor and Francis, for the British Cartographic Society; subscription journal with optional paid open access, not confirmed on the journal's own page). Published the strain analysis of Boutoura and Livieratos (1986); suits the method (one common fit, relative error, falsification baseline, projection sensitivity). Word limit: not confirmed (the society page at cartography.org.uk refers authors to the Editorial Manager instructions, which could not be loaded); check before submitting.
3. **e-Perimetron** (online, non-profit; Thessaloniki). Publishes cartometric studies of early maps (Balletti 2006; Boutoura and Livieratos 2006; Livieratos 2006b). A natural home for the method and the distortion grids. Its home page describes it as a non-profit journal and states no author fees; the contributions page could not be loaded, so length rules and the absence of fees are not confirmed.
4. **Journal of Historical Geography** (Elsevier; hybrid open access, so the subscription route carries no APC; the guide mentions no page or submission charges). Guide for authors (ScienceDirect): standard articles "should generally not exceed 10000 words, including all references and an abstract of under 200 words"; tables and figures do not count. The paper fits the length but the abstract (240 words) would need cutting to under 200, and it would need a stronger historical framing of the eight maps and less statistical detail.

## Summary of the contribution (for a cover letter)

Cartometric studies of single historical maps are common, but their numbers cannot be compared, because each uses its own transformation, projection and error measure. This paper re-fits the control points of eight maps made between about AD 150 and 1821 (Ptolemy's Geography, the Peutinger Table, al-Idrisi, the Carta Pisana, the Hereford Mappa Mundi, the Gough Map, Saxton and Ino) with one documented method pre-specified in a written plan: Helmert fits in a common projection, error relative to the size of the area mapped, a neighbourhood measure of shape, bootstrap intervals, MapAnalyst-style distortion grids, a falsification baseline and pre-specified checks. Relative errors run from 1.7% (Ino, as compiled in 1878) to 26% (Peutinger). A negative association with date rests on the two surveys and is not robust to the ground projection; before 1500 there is no clear trend. Errors are regional and, on balance, grow away from each map's centre. The results support reading map accuracy through purpose rather than progress. Data and code are released.
